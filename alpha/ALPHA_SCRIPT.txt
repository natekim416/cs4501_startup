[SLIDE 1 - HOOK - 0:00-0:35]
"Storage Almost Full." One developer ran a cleanup tool on their
laptop and found fifty-eight gigabytes in a hundred and twenty-eight
old node_modules folders. Most of them can be rebuilt with one command.
Almost nobody deletes them, because deleting the wrong folder the night
before a deadline costs you your project. We're Cleanasaurus, and we
make that deletion safe.

[SLIDE 2 - MVP VALIDATION - 0:35-1:20]
Our MVP hypothesis was that students would let an AI clean their files
if every plan was checked first. We planned fifteen testers and ran ten,
five CS and five non-CS, from October thirtieth to November second. We
scored them against the thresholds from our pitch. Safety held: zero
unsafe actions. But our primary KPI failed. Only thirty percent ran a
full plan in their first session; seventy percent ran at least eighty
percent of one. Repeat use and undo landed in between. And testers got
back about two percent of the space they wanted. The app said it freed
twelve gigabytes, and zero bytes left the disk. So our hypothesis isn't
validated as built, and we pivoted.

[SLIDE 3 - PIVOT - 1:20-2:00]
Every CS tester told us where their space actually was: build folders.
The median CS tester had thirty-eight gigabytes of rebuildable clutter,
against one point four of duplicates. So we kept the trust engine and
aimed it there. To be honest about competition: CleanMyMac's new command
line tool already deletes node_modules older than a week. What nobody
does is prove a folder is safe to delete, with its lockfile, no
uncommitted work, and no secrets inside, and save the command to rebuild
it. And nobody lets you just say "I'm done with CS 3140."

Q&A: HARD QUESTIONS AND HONEST ANSWERS

Q: "CleanMyMac adds a chat box next quarter. What's your moat?"
A: Their purge deletes by age alone. We prove each folder is rebuildable
   and save the rebuild command, and Claude maps what you say to the
   right projects. The head-to-head KPI in our Beta test measures
   whether students actually prefer that.

Q: "What decision did Claude make that a regex couldn't?"
A: Plain requests like "done with CS 3140" we could handle with a regex,
   and our offline planner does. "I'm done with my machine learning
   class" it can't: it has to ask. Claude maps it to cs4774-ml. That's
   one of five live tests against the real model, and all five pass,
   including "I'm NOT done with CS 3140" and an ambiguous "the CS
   class," where Claude asks instead of guessing. Safety never depends
   on it: the validator rechecks every folder, and in one live test it
   did reject Claude's first plan.

Q: "Your tests are all AI-generated. Did you review them?"
A: Claude reviewed them, and an independent AI review found four weak
   tests. Human review is scheduled, and we report zero percent
   human-refined until it happens.

Q: "Why does the red run show 'module not found' instead of failed assertions?"
A: The first red run failed because the module didn't exist yet. That's
   weaker evidence, and we say so in our TDD log. The Claude-path tests
   we added later failed on real assertions before we wrote the code.

Q: "Your slide says 30% but your report says 70%."
A: Two definitions. Thirty percent ran the whole plan, which is the KPI
   we pitched, and it fails our forty percent line. Seventy percent ran
   at least eighty percent of a plan. We report the strict one.

Q: "Why was a simulated study uploaded and deleted?"
A: A teammate uploaded a draft simulation by mistake. We pulled it
   because a simulation isn't evidence from real users.

Q: "Your primary KPI failed. Why pivot instead of calling it invalidated?"
A: The part that failed was value, not safety. Zero unsafe actions, and
   seventy percent ran most of a plan. What they couldn't do was get real
   space back, because the MVP could only remove exact copies and freed
   zero bytes. Every CS tester pointed at build folders, so we kept the
   safety engine and changed what it cleans. Beta tests that bet, with
   pass/fail targets on slide 7.
