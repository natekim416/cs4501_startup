[SLIDE 1 - HOOK - 0:00-0:25]
"Storage Almost Full." One developer ran a cleanup tool on their
laptop and found fifty-eight gigabytes in a hundred and twenty-eight
old node_modules folders. Most of it rebuilds with one command. Almost
nobody deletes them, because deleting the wrong folder the night before
a deadline costs you your project. We're Cleanasaurus, and we make that
cleanup safe.

[SLIDE 2 - MVP VALIDATION - 0:25-1:09]
Our MVP bet was that students would let an AI clean their files if
every plan was checked first. We tested ten people, five CS students and
five friends and family. Here's what went wrong. Only thirty percent ran a full plan in their first
session, below our forty percent fail line, though seventy percent ran
most of one. Only thirty percent came back on their own. Undo was
all-or-nothing, and one tester's own app got flagged as malware. The
biggest miss was space: testers got back about two percent of what they
wanted. Our counter said twelve gigabytes freed, and zero bytes left the
disk. Safety held. Value didn't.

[SLIDE 3 - PIVOT - 1:09-2:00]
So why pivot to developers? We asked testers where their space
actually goes. Seven of ten said: somewhere our app couldn't reach. That
was all five of our CS students. (Point at "5 of 5".)
Their space was in build folders. That's the stuff coding tools download
for you: node_modules, Python environments, Xcode data, AI
models. (Point at the table.) The typical CS tester had thirty-eight
gigabytes of it, and only about one and a half of duplicate files. That's
twenty-seven times more.
And all of it is rebuildable: delete it, and one command brings it back.
That's what makes it safe to clean. CS students also trusted us most;
non-CS testers undid over a third of their actions.
Other tools delete these just because they're old. We only delete
what we can prove comes back.

Q&A: HARD QUESTIONS AND HONEST ANSWERS

Q: "Why is the demo a video?"
A: So the three minutes are reliable on classroom Wi-Fi. The app is on
   this laptop, and we can run it live right now if you'd like.

Q: "What decision did Claude make that a regex couldn't?"
A: In the video, one sentence became a whole plan: which class each file
   belongs to, which copies to remove, and to leave the resumes alone.
   In our five live tests on the real model, "I'm done with my machine
   learning class" maps to the right project, which our offline rules
   can't match, and "the CS class" makes Claude ask which one instead of
   guessing. Safety never depends on Claude: plain code rechecks every
   action, and in one live test it rejected Claude's first plan.

Q: "CleanMyMac adds a chat box next quarter. What's your moat?"
A: Their purge deletes by age alone. We prove each folder is rebuildable
   and save the rebuild command, and Claude maps what you say to the
   right projects. Our Beta measures whether CS students actually prefer
   that, head to head.

Q: "Your tests are all AI-generated. Did you review them?"
A: Claude reviewed them, and an independent AI review found four weak
   tests. We also tested the app by hand this week; three bugs we found
   that way each became a failing test before the fix. No one has
   hand-edited a test yet; that review is scheduled, and we report it as
   zero until it happens.

Q: "Why does the red run show 'module not found' instead of failed assertions?"
A: The first red run failed because the module didn't exist yet. That's
   weaker evidence, and we say so in our TDD log. The Claude-path tests
   we added later failed on real assertions before we wrote the code.

Q: "Your slide says 30% but your report says 70%."
A: Two definitions. Thirty percent ran the whole plan, which is the KPI
   we pitched, and it fails our forty percent line. Seventy percent ran
   at least eighty percent of a plan. We report the strict one.

Q: "Why was a simulated study uploaded and deleted?"
A: A teammate uploaded a draft simulation by mistake. We pulled it
   because a simulation isn't evidence from real users.

Q: "Your primary KPI failed. Why pivot instead of calling it invalidated?"
A: The part that failed was value, not safety. Zero unsafe actions, and
   seventy percent ran most of a plan. What they couldn't do was get real
   space back, because the MVP could only remove exact copies and freed
   zero bytes. Every CS tester pointed at build folders, so we kept the
   safety engine and changed what it cleans. Beta tests that bet, with
   pass lines on slide 7.
